# Paper Mills: The Dark Side of Academic Publishing

Somewhere in the published literature there is a paper discussing "bosom peril." Elsewhere there is one on "counterfeit consciousness." Neither phrase means anything, and both got past peer review, and once you know why, a fair amount of what is wrong with academic publishing falls into place.

## What a paper mill actually is

A paper mill writes fake manuscripts, fabricates the data to go in them, and sells authorship slots to whoever will pay. You do not do the research. You buy a position on the author list of something that was never done, and in due course it appears in a journal with an ISSN and an impact factor.

The scale is the part that is hard to hold in your head. Retraction Watch put the number of paper mill articles that have made it into the literature over the past two decades at 400,000 or more (Retraction Watch, 2023), and retractions attributable to mills went from 10 in 2019 to 2,099 in 2023 (The Conversation, 2025). Those are the ones somebody caught.

It is also a real industry with revenue. The UK Research Integrity Office estimates the global trade at something like $10 million, and a single Russian operation would have cleared $6.5 million between 2019 and 2021 if it had sold every slot it produced (The Conversation, 2025).

## How they work

A mill does not write a paper and hope. The manuscript is the easy part: templates, paraphrased versions of existing work, and increasingly text generated by a language model that reads plausibly enough for a tired reviewer. Data gets invented, images get manipulated, and entire studies get described that never took place.

The interesting engineering is downstream, in the part that guarantees the thing is accepted. Mills have been caught paying journal editors, with offers running as high as $20,000 (Science, 2024). They also work through brokers who control editorial decisions at particular journals, which is what lets them place batches of fraudulent papers at once rather than one at a time.

Some of them do not even hide. The Academic Research and Development Association in India advertises "journal publication" services openly, with a list of journals where acceptance is guaranteed (Richardson et al., 2025). When one of those journals gets deindexed from Scopus or Web of Science for suspicious output, ARDA updates the list and moves on. The business survives the loss of any particular journal because the journals were never the point.

## Tortured phrases

Which brings us back to bosom peril. One of the most effective ways to spot this material is to look for what Guillaume Cabanac and colleagues at Toulouse named tortured phrases: the wreckage left behind when text goes through paraphrasing software.

The software swaps words for synonyms with no idea what any of them mean in context. So "breast cancer" comes out as "bosom peril." "Mean temperature" becomes "cruel temperature." "Signal to noise" becomes "flag to clamor" (Cabanac et al., 2021). My favourite is "glucose bigotry," which started life as glucose intolerance.

The purpose is to get past plagiarism detection, and it works, because the detectors compare strings. What comes out the other side is grammatically clean and semantically dead, which no reviewer who read the sentence could miss. That these papers are published tells you how many sentences in the literature are not being read.

Cabanac's group turned the observation into the Problematic Paper Screener, which scans published work for tortured phrases and other markers automatically (Cabanac et al., 2021). By September 2025 it had catalogued more than 7,500 distinct tortured phrases and flagged over 15,000 suspect papers. Of those, 2,760 had been retracted (Chemistry World, 2025). The gap between flagged and retracted is its own story.

## Why anyone buys

Publish or perish, still, after everything.

Career advancement in most academic systems runs on publication counts, and the requirement has spread well beyond the people who were trained to do research. Doctors need publications for promotion. So do teachers at vocational schools. More than half of medical residents in China admit to misconduct of some kind, buying papers among it (Science, 2024), and the reason is not mysterious: publications remain the cheapest route to promotion for people who have neither the training nor the hours to produce them honestly.

This is not a story about one country. Russia, India, Iran and others run research policies that reward volume, and the customer base is global: researchers in Indonesia, Malaysia, Germany and the United States have all been caught buying (The Conversation, 2025). Some universities now require undergraduates to publish as coursework. One researcher summarised the result for Science: "Students are really desperate to get research papers in whichever way possible. No one really cares about the outcomes. It's all about outputs" (Science, 2024).

## The detection problem is getting worse

Large language models arrived at roughly the moment the community was getting competent at spotting the older kind of fraud, and they have made production cheap and the output much harder to flag. The text is trained on human writing and reads like it; the detectors built for template reuse and paraphrasing artefacts are aimed at a problem that is being solved around them.

Jennifer Byrne, who has spent years on this, notes that the papers themselves are changing: "We have found that now increasingly the papers are getting much more complex, or at least the ones that we study are getting more complex" (The Scientist, 2024). Reese Richardson at Northwestern has put a number on where that leads: "You can see a scenario in a decade or less where you could have more than half of studies being published each year being fraudulent" (Chemistry World, 2025).

The structural advantage sits with the mills. They can abandon a tactic the moment it stops working and switch research areas overnight, because nothing they produce depends on anyone knowing the field.

## What it costs

The literature is a shared resource and this contaminates it. Researchers who build on a fabricated result spend months and grant money reaching a dead end, and never find out why. Clinicians making decisions from synthesised data can harm patients. The premise that work accumulates, that you can stand on what came before, stops holding when a measurable fraction of what came before did not happen.

It is also straightforwardly unfair. Someone with a short and honest publication record loses the job or the grant to someone whose record was purchased, and the selection pressure runs in the wrong direction for as long as nobody checks.

There is public trust to lose too, at a point where we are asking the public to act on scientific advice about climate, pandemics and technologies nobody has lived with yet.

And there is money. Public universities fund research and publication incentives out of taxes and tuition. Every euro that ends up paying for a fabricated paper is a euro that did not go to work that was actually done (The Conversation, 2025).

## What might help

Publishers have started screening properly: automated checks for image manipulation, for the same manuscript submitted to several journals at once, for tortured phrases and the other markers. The STM Integrity Hub lets publishers compare notes on suspicious submissions before anything is published rather than after.

That is necessary and it is not sufficient, and Luis Nunes Amaral at Northwestern says why: "You cannot have a system where you are trying to detect fraud after it's created. You actually have to prevent people from putting these things into the system" (Chemistry World, 2025).

Prevention means the incentives, which means the thing nobody wants to touch. As long as scientists are assessed on how many papers they produce rather than what is in them, there will be a market for papers. Some argue for going after the buyers harder. Richardson argues for the structural version: "We need to make the scientific community much less competitive, fairer and more equal. Inequality, locally and globally, has led to this problem" (Chemistry World, 2025).

The cheapest intervention is simply telling people. A surprising number of academics and PhD students have never heard of paper mills and have no idea what the warning signs look like. You cannot report what you cannot recognise.

## Where this leaves us

Paper mills are large, well capitalised and adaptive, and they are growing faster than the countermeasures. Treating them as a detection problem means permanently playing the second move.

Fixing it means changing how scientific work is evaluated and rewarded, which is slow, political and unpopular with everyone who does well under the current arrangement. Until that happens the mills will keep operating, because the demand is real and we created it.

In the meantime: if you come across a paper worried about bosom peril, you know what you are looking at. Flag it.
