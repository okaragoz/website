# The Dual Face of Scientific Ethics: Navigating Responsibilities Between Laboratory and Society

Every so often a result leaves the building and stops being yours. Someone else reads it, uses it, funds the next step or kills it, and the question of who is answerable for what happens next turns out to have no settled answer. The scientist who did the work? The society that paid for it and now has to live with it? In practice it is both, and the awkward part is that nobody can say where one ends and the other begins.

## Freedom, and the bill that comes with it

For most of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries there was a comfortable position available, and plenty of people took it. Science produces knowledge; knowledge is neither good nor bad; what anyone does with it afterwards is not the scientist's department (EMBO Reports, 2005). It is a tidy arrangement. If nuclear physics made the bomb possible, that was a problem for politics, not for physics.

I do not think anybody can hold that position with a straight face now. Public money pays for the lab. Public institutions house it, train the people in it and lend them the authority that makes their results count. Accepting that and then claiming to be a neutral bystander is not a philosophical stance, it is an accounting error. Darryl (2006) puts it more carefully, in terms of the trust and privilege society extends to researchers, but the arithmetic is the same.

Accepting the responsibility is easy. Discharging it is where the trouble starts, because the dilemmas are genuinely hard and they arrive early, at the point where you are still choosing what to work on (Resnik & Elliott, 2015). Should a virologist publish work that speeds up a vaccine when the same work shortens the path to a weapon? Should someone studying memory and fear avoid a line of research that would make interrogation more effective? These are not seminar questions. For a lot of people they are a Tuesday.

Dual-use research is the name for the bind: legitimate work whose results can be turned into a threat to public health or security (NSABB, 2007). The example I keep returning to is from 2022, when a group ran their own drug-discovery model in reverse. The system was built to search chemical space for therapeutic molecules while screening out toxicity; they flipped the toxicity filter into a target. In under six hours it generated roughly 40,000 candidate molecules, including VX and a set of compounds the authors had never seen before (Urbina et al., 2022). Nobody synthesised anything. They were testing what the tool would do if asked nicely, and the answer arrived over a lunch break.

## What society puts in, and what it does with the result

The public side of this relationship is no more consistent. We want innovation aimed at the large problems, climate and disease and energy, and we are uneasy about most of the change innovation actually delivers. Darryl (2006) notes the oddity that a public with no particular scientific training still manages to arrive at reasonably stable collective positions, accepting animal cloning while rejecting human reproductive cloning, by routes that are hard to reconstruct afterwards.

The underlying bargain is not complicated. Society funds the education and the research and grants an unusual degree of autonomy; researchers produce knowledge and look after it. Shamoo and Resnik (2014) add the part that tends to get left out, which is that the bargain is enforced by consequences: researchers who cause harm, or who had the chance to do good and did not, spend down a reserve of public support that none of us owns individually.

What gets forgotten more often is that the obligation runs both ways. A company that buries an inconvenient trial, a government that turns a finding into a weapon, an industry that waves off its own environmental data: none of those is a failure of science. Scientists made the tool. Somebody else chose the target. Working out which uses of knowledge a society is prepared to live with is a conversation that has to happen outside the laboratory, and it mostly does not happen at all until something has already gone wrong (EMBO Reports, 2005).

## Where the boundaries blur

The hardest ground is where the two sets of obligations overlap and pull against each other. Three cases come up again and again, and none of them has a clean resolution.

**Publishing against security.** Should a journal run a result that could enable harm? Kelley (2006) makes both halves of the argument and refuses to pick: good intentions do not discharge your responsibility for what a malicious reader does with the method, and restricting access erodes the transparency that a publicly funded institution in an open society depends on. Both are true. They do not reconcile, and every editor handling a dual-use manuscript is deciding the case on its facts without a rule to fall back on.

**Private money against public interest.** Universities push their researchers towards industry funding and patents, and researchers increasingly hold patents, equity or consultancies alongside their academic posts (EMBO Reports, 2005). The line between public knowledge and private product has not so much moved as dissolved. Who owns a result that public funding produced and a company commercialised? When the commercial interest and the public health interest point in opposite directions, whose call is it?

**Global against local.** Knowledge does not stop at borders, but regulation does. A technique prohibited in one country can be pursued legally in the next, and the consequences of either choice land everywhere, as pandemics, environmental damage and biological weapons all demonstrate (Hurst, 2024). Our ethical frameworks are still built around national jurisdictions for a problem that has not respected them in decades.

## Rules, outcomes, and neither being enough

These cases also expose how little help the standard moral frameworks offer. Judging an action purely by its consequences requires knowing the consequences, which is exactly what you do not have when the same result cures and kills depending on who picks it up. Judging by rules and duties fails differently: the rules were written for situations somebody had already encountered, and these are not those situations.

What has stayed with me is closer to virtue ethics, built around integrity and practical judgement. Darryl (2006) describes researchers as carrying obligations at three levels at once: for the integrity of their own work, towards the people they work with and supervise, and as representatives of the profession to everyone outside it. Read that way, ethics in science is less a checklist than a skill, and the skill is partly the ability to notice the moment when the checklist has run out.

## What would actually help

If the boundary cannot be drawn cleanly, drawing it may be the wrong project. The useful work is in building the machinery for carrying the responsibility jointly.

Researchers can treat ethics as part of the design rather than something applied afterwards, and can bring in people who actually know the territory. Resnik and Elliott (2015) suggest collaborating with colleagues in ethics, politics or policy, which sounds obvious and almost never happens, partly because there is no line in the budget for it.

Institutions can build somewhere for the argument to take place that is not a compliance form. UNESCO (2019) makes the point that the old habit of separating method from values no longer survives contact with how research is actually done and funded. An ethics committee made of scientists and bioethicists is a start; one that also includes people from the communities the work touches is a different and more uncomfortable thing, which is roughly the argument for it.

Societies can accept that restraining a line of research is sometimes right and always costs something. A survey of life scientists found many already acting on perceived misuse risk without being told to, in the absence of any regulation requiring it (NRC & AAAS, 2009). That suggests the useful move is to build on the judgement already in the field rather than reach first for blanket prohibition, while keeping democratic oversight genuinely in the room.

And training has to cover it. Resnik and Elliott (2015) argue for giving social responsibility real time in research-conduct courses, on the grounds that the questions are not clear-cut and take practice. I would go further: the people coming up need practice at explaining themselves to a public that has every right to ask, and they get almost none.

## A line that keeps moving

I have stopped thinking of the boundary between scientific and societal responsibility as a border and started thinking of it as a negotiation that never closes. Every capability shifts it. Every unforeseen consequence shifts it back. The goal was never a final allocation of blame; it was keeping the conversation running while the ground moves.

Jones (2007) argues that scientific communities need to state the terms of their social contract explicitly, and that writing it down is what keeps public trust intact. That takes some honesty about what science cannot do and does not yet know, some restraint about how far scientific authority actually reaches, and enough nerve to engage with ethical questions that will not resolve.

Science is the best instrument we have for understanding the world and changing it, and an instrument like that asks for judgement as well as competence: not only what can be done, but what should be, and who gets a say in deciding. Neither scientists nor society produces that judgement alone. It comes out of the conversation between them, which is uncomfortable by design and does not have an off switch.
