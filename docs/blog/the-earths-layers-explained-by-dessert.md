# The Earth’s Layers as a Dessert?

<AudioPlayer src="/audio/2025/04/earth-layers-dessert.mp3" title="Listen to this article" />

Some numbers refuse to fit inside my head. The core of the Sun at 15 million °C. The Earth at 4.6 billion years old. A nanoparticle at a billionth of a metre. I can write them down and do arithmetic with them, and I cannot feel any of them, because they are too far outside anything I have touched. What is strange is that one of the least intuitive parts of the planet is not distant or microscopic at all. It is about thirty kilometres below my feet.

We walk on it, build on it, tunnel through it, and almost never ask what it is made of. "Solid ground" is treated as a complete answer. But what is actually down there thirty kilometres below, and what is going on a few hundred kilometres further where no drill has ever reached, where the rock is hot enough to flow and everything happens at the pace of continental drift?

That region is the **lithosphere**, the outer shell of the Earth: the crust plus the cold uppermost part of the mantle. It is not a lid. It bends, it breaks, it flows under the right conditions, and it **moves**. It is cracked into plates, some the size of continents, and they slide past each other, collide, pull apart and occasionally sink back into the mantle. Every earthquake, every volcanic eruption and every mountain range comes out of what that shell is doing.

Which leaves the practical problem of how you picture something you cannot see, touch or sample. You cannot open the Earth and take out a piece of crust and mantle to watch how they behave. We work with seismic waves, rock mechanics experiments, GPS and gravity data instead, and I spend a fair amount of time running the same question in a tank of silicone. But to think about the lithosphere as a system, I usually need something cruder and faster, and the crude fast version in this case comes from the dessert menu.

* * *

### Dessert Time: Edible Earth Models

These are not teaching aids somebody invented for a museum. They are the names working geologists use in papers, and the argument between them is a real argument about how strength is distributed with depth.

![](/images/2025/04/Screenshot-2024-01-30-at-15.10.24_upscayl_2x_upscayl-standard-4x--1-.png)Adapted from Bürgmann and Dresen (2008), this figure illustrates the end-member types of lithospheric strength profiles discussed in their study. Differential stress is used here as a proxy for rock strength.

### **The Crème Brûlée Model**

A crisp brittle sugar crust over soft custard. The crust stands for a strong upper crust, or sometimes the whole crust, sitting on a weak upper mantle. Tap the sugar lightly and it flexes, which is elastic behaviour. Press harder and it fractures, which is brittle failure, and that transition is roughly where earthquakes come from.

This picture gets used for tectonically active regions, where the mantle is taken to be the weak layer under a strong crust. There are variants depending on whether only the upper crust is strong or the entire crust is, and what they share is the weak upper mantle underneath. It has been used to explain crustal strength profiles across a number of geodynamic studies (e.g., Bürgmann and Dresen, 2008; Jackson et al., 2008).

* * *

### **The Jelly Sandwich Model**

More layers, and a different weak spot. Two strong slices of bread, the upper crust and the lithospheric mantle, with a wobbly jelly filling between them: the lower crust. The claim is that the lower crust is the mechanically weak layer, deforming more readily than the strong material above and below. Under stress the jelly stretches and then gives way while the bread bends and holds.

Chen and Molnar proposed it in 1983, and Burov and Watts developed it further in 2006. It is still the standard picture for continental deformation, particularly where the lower crust may be decoupling the upper crust from the mantle beneath. That decoupling matters more than it sounds: a weak layer in the middle is what allows the top of the crust to move more or less independently of what is underneath it, and whether such a layer exists is one of the questions my own work keeps running into.

* * *

### **The Banana Split Model**

If the other two feel too neatly layered for the actual mess of tectonics, this one is the mess. A fragmented lithosphere: blocks of crust and mantle as the scoops, separated by deep fault zones as the melted, fluid-filled gaps between them. As the ice cream softens at those edges, the boundaries weaken, which is close to what real plates do along transform faults and rift zones.

Fluid matters here, water above all. It lowers rock strength and speeds up deformation, and it concentrates at plate boundaries, which is part of why deformation concentrates there too. I find this the most useful picture for the long-lived structures, the San Andreas or the Alpine Fault in New Zealand, where the interesting physics is all in the boundary rather than in the blocks.

* * *

### So which one is right?

None of them, which is the point of having three. Each is an end member, and which one you reach for depends on the region, the data in front of you and what you are trying to work out. Real lithosphere does not have to pick one.

That is also why they are worth something. They are not conclusions, they are starting positions. They let you sketch a hypothesis before building a model, they give students a way to hold forces they will never see, and they make arguments between researchers faster, because once everyone is using the same picture you can get straight to disagreeing about the part that matters.

No dessert is going to replace a seismic model or a thermomechanical simulation. What they do is make the structure graspable long enough to ask a better question about it, and I have never found a more efficient way to explain mechanical layering to someone who does not work on it.

They are also, as these things go, quite good fun.
