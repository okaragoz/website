# Venus's Molten Fault Lines: How Magma Lubrication Shapes a Planet's Giant Cracks

Venus gets called Earth's sister planet on the strength of its size, mass and bulk composition. The surface is a different matter: around 460 °C, no water anywhere, and a tectonic style that does not resemble ours.

This study followed a set of very large fault zones across Eastern Aphrodite Terra, a highland region near the equator. Eight of them, between 218 and 706 kilometres long. The interesting part is not their size but their angle: they slip at dips that, with ordinary rock friction, should have locked them solid. Something is reducing the friction, and on a planet with no water it is unlikely to be water.

## Lopsided valleys

Eastern Aphrodite Terra is cut by chasmata, deep valleys up to three kilometres deep running for thousands of kilometres. They are usually described as rift valleys, and the first thing the Magellan radar shows is that they are strikingly asymmetric: one flank slopes gently at about 5°, the other plunges at up to 40°.

Along those steep slopes are the shear zones, where large blocks of crust have ground past each other. The faults dip at 25° to 37°, which is shallow, and they dip in the opposite direction from the steep slope that exposes them. Standing at the foot of the cliff, you would expect the fault behind it to dip back into the hillside. These tilt forward, away from it.

In places the fault planes are partly exposed as terraces cut into the steep slope. Those terraces are what reveal that the faults moved twice, in opposite directions.

## Thrust first, then normal

Comparing these straight chasmata with the curved trenches that wrap around the nearby coronae gives a two-phase history.

They began as thrust faults, under compression. The asymmetric valley profile, steep on the inside and gentle on the outside, is what thrust faulting produces, and it resembles the geometry of a subduction zone.

The stress regime then reversed and the same faults reactivated as normal faults, the hanging wall dropping and peeling open parts of the fault surface into the terraces we can see. Structural geology calls this negative inversion, and on Venus it means the regional regime swung from convergent to extensional.

## The friction problem

Normal faulting at 25° to 37° is the difficulty. Crustal rock typically has a friction coefficient of 0.6 to 0.8, and to slip as a normal fault at angles that shallow you need something closer to 0.2.

On Earth the answer is almost always water: fluid pressure in the fault zone pushes back against the weight of the rock above and lets the fault slide. Venus has no surface water, and at 460 °C anything in the shallow crust would have gone long ago.

## What sits on top of the faults

The evidence for the alternative is on the surface, directly above the fault zones. Working with both standard and inverted radar, two things stand out.

Radar-smooth plains: dark patches up to 300 kilometres long that pool into local lows and drape over the exposed fault terraces. They scatter almost no signal back, which means they are very smooth, and the way they flow across the landscape implies the material had low viscosity when it was emplaced.

Rugged terrain: small isolated hills with very rough surfaces, often sitting in the middle of those smooth plains, consistent with a mixture of fault breccia and solidified melt.

Microwave emissivity differs measurably between the hanging wall, the footwall, the smooth plains and the rugged terrain, so these are genuinely different materials rather than the same rock at different roughness.

## Where the melt comes from

**Frictional melting** is real and Venus should be good at it. Its rocks start at 450 °C, much closer to their melting point; they are dry, and dry rocks are stronger and generate more heat when they slip; and they are mafic, so they melt at lower temperatures than quartz-rich continental rock. Our estimate is that Venus produces roughly four times the frictional melt Earth would for a fault of the same size.

It still is not enough. Those volumes are centimetres to perhaps a metre thick, which is invisible at Magellan's 75-metre resolution. Frictional melting almost certainly glazes these fault planes. It cannot build a 300-kilometre smooth plain.

**Magma from below** can. Eastern Aphrodite Terra is scattered with volcanic edifices and sits near several large coronae, which are raised by mantle plumes and have partly molten interiors that work as reservoirs. An active fault develops a damage zone of fractures around its main plane, and those open pathways for magma to move upward under the pressure gradients along the fault. As the fault moves, a slurry of magma and broken rock is driven up until it reaches the surface: the magma, being runnier, spreads out as the smooth plains, and the coarser fraction piles into the rugged hills.

## Why it keeps going

Whichever source dominates, the mechanical consequence is the same: melt on a fault plane removes most of the friction. A liquid has essentially none, and a thin film is enough to let the two walls slide. That is how a low-angle normal fault works on a planet with no water.

It is also self-sustaining. Movement opens fractures that tap a reservoir, or generates melt directly; the melt coats the plane and drops the friction; the lower friction allows continued movement at a shallow angle; and that movement brings up more melt.

## A planet that is not sitting still

The smooth plains and rugged terrain look crisp and unweathered, which suggests they are geologically young. A few areas carry what appear to be small landslide deposits, plausibly shaken loose by seismicity along these faults.

If the faults are as young as they look, they may still be capable of moving. Fault scaling relationships, which tie fault length to maximum magnitude, would put structures of this size in the magnitude 7 to 8 range, with the caveat that scaling laws calibrated on Earth carry real uncertainty when exported.

The compression-to-extension reversal is the broader point: Venus has no plate tectonics and still undergoes substantial crustal deformation driven by mantle plumes, and the regime it deforms under can change fundamentally over time.

## Why strain localises at all

Underneath this is a general question: why does deformation gather into narrow fault zones instead of spreading across the whole crust?

On Earth water usually does it, weakening rock chemically and lowering friction through pore pressure. Venus has none, which forces the question of what else can.

The answer this work points to is melt. On a hot, dry planet, melt lubrication may occupy the role water occupies here, which makes the coupling between faulting and magmatism central rather than incidental to how such a planet deforms.

That has a bearing beyond Venus. The early Earth was much hotter and its crust probably behaved more like this. Some of the oldest fault zones on Earth may have worked the same way, with melt doing the lubricating long before plate tectonics in its modern form existed.

## What would settle it

Magellan's 75 metres per pixel resolves these faults and not much more. It cannot show striations on a fault surface, which would give the slip direction directly, and it cannot track small changes in roughness.

VERITAS and EnVision, both planned for the 2030s, carry radar well beyond Magellan's. They should be able to confirm or refute this interpretation, and if any of these structures are still active, to catch them at it.
