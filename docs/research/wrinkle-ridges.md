# What Ancient Ridge Formations Tell Us About the Red Planet's Crust

Look at enough images of Mars and the ridges start to stand out: long, sinuous features running for hundreds of kilometres across the plains. They are called wrinkle ridges, and they form when the crust is squeezed horizontally until it buckles and thrusts up along a fault. Their shape is therefore not decorative. It is a record of how the crust responded to being compressed, and with enough care it can be read backwards.

This study looked at Lunae Planum, a plain on the eastern flank of the Tharsis rise. Tharsis is the volcanic bulge that pushed up out of the planet billions of years ago, and Lunae Planum is part of what had to absorb that. The questions were narrow and answerable: how much did the crust shorten, how deep do the faults go, and what does that imply about the structure they are cutting through.

## What the measurements show

Six major ridges were analysed using high-resolution imagery and digital elevation models built from stereo pairs. The first result is a clean gradient from west to east. The westernmost ridge averages about 78 metres high and 5,650 metres wide. The easternmost is 38 metres high and 4,300 metres wide. Deformation falls off with distance from Tharsis, which is what you would expect if Tharsis is the thing doing the pushing.

Pairing those measurements with kinematic models of how rocks fold above a fault turns the heights into shortening. Western Lunae Planum took about 116 metres of horizontal shortening, the eastern part about 56 metres. Those are small numbers for a planetary process, and they are still enough to constrain what lies beneath.

## The detachment

The more useful result is what the modelling says about the faults. They do not run straight down into the crust. They curve and flatten into a near-horizontal detachment, and that detachment sits about four kilometres deep in the west and about 2.6 kilometres deep in the east.

A detachment that tilts gently toward Tharsis, under topography that also slopes gently toward Tharsis, is a critical taper: mechanically the same configuration as a fold-and-thrust belt on Earth. The Jura and the Zagros are built the same way. That a planet with no plate tectonics produces the same geometry suggests the mechanics are not specific to Earth.

## Why there would be a weak layer at that depth

A detachment needs something weak to slide on, and 2.6 to 4 kilometres is a specific enough depth to ask what was there. On ancient Mars that interval is where liquid water could exist: below the frozen ground, above the point where the rock closes up. Water trapped there, under the full weight of the rock above it, raises the pore pressure and weakens the rock enough for a fault to run along it.

The region supports this from another direction. Kasei Valles, one of the large outflow channels, cuts down to comparable depths nearby, and channels like it are thought to form when pressurised groundwater escapes catastrophically. The detachment and the outflow channel may be two expressions of the same aquifer, one where it held and one where it failed.

## What is still open

Whether the water accumulated before the ridges grew or alongside them is not resolved by this work. Neither is what the fluid actually was: pure water and brine behave differently, and the difference matters for how weak the layer gets. Nor is the growth rate of the ridges, which would need an independent clock.

Subsurface sounding is the way to settle it. InSight has already given a first direct look at the Martian interior through seismology, and radar or seismic coverage over regions like this one would test the detachment depth directly rather than inferring it from the surface.

## How it was done

The elevation models came from Context Camera stereo pairs. Morphometric analysis ran over more than 500 topographic profiles, and the fold geometry was handled with fault-propagation fold theory following Suppe and Medwedeff. The constraint that holds the whole reconstruction together came from places where impact craters and valley walls cut through a ridge and expose the fault plane itself, giving a dip of 38° ± 5° measured rather than assumed.
